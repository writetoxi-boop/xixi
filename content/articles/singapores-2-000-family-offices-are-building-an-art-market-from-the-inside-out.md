The city now hosts approximately 2,000 single-family offices — a nearly 4% increase since 2020. The principals of these offices are time-poor, globally mobile, and increasingly interested in art not as a bohemian luxury but as a sophisticated asset class. They bring to it exactly what they bring to every other investment category: a demand for stability, logistical excellence, transparent governance, and — increasingly — civic legibility.

Art SG 2026 was the clearest proof of the thesis. The strategic decision to fold SEA Focus into the main fair floor at Marina Bay Sands was, as one observer put it, a masterstroke of efficiency. But the more significant development was the inauguration of the Tanoto Art Foundation at New Bahru, alongside the expansion of the Sam Art SG Fund to S$250,000. These are not speculative bets. They are anchor investments — the kind that signal a commitment to building something that lasts beyond a market cycle.

Singapore's critics say it lacks the gritty soul of a real art capital. This misreads what is being built here.

The city is not trying to be London or New York or even Hong Kong. It is trying to be something that does not yet have a name: a stable, infrastructure-rich, legally transparent cultural hub where serious capital can sit alongside serious art without either compromising the other. Singapore billionaires increased their wealth by two-thirds in a single year, reaching $258.8 billion — the third-largest pool of wealth in Asia-Pacific after China and India.

That capital is looking for a home. Art is one of the more interesting addresses.
