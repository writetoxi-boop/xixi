For context, that is roughly comparable to the entire GDP of a mid-sized European economy. Generated by a single country's creative sector. In eleven months.

And yet: ask anyone outside Indonesia to name an Indonesian fashion brand. Watch the pause.

This is the central paradox of Indonesian fashion in 2026. The country is one of the world's most significant garment producers, a UNESCO-recognised custodian of batik as intangible cultural heritage, and home to a generation of designers — Peggy Hartanto, Ria Miranda, Tex Saverio, the teams behind Imaji Studio and Alleira Batik — who are producing work that belongs in international conversations about luxury, craft, and contemporary design. Batik has moved off ceremonial cloth and onto accessories, bags, and wearable contemporary art that international buyers are actively seeking out.

But the brand architecture hasn't caught up. Indonesia exports the product. It has not yet exported the story.

The government is trying to fix this. The 2026 Inacraft — the largest handicraft trade fair in Southeast Asia — is being positioned as a hexahelix ecosystem play: government, industry, academia, community, media, finance all pointing in the same direction. Digital adoption, IP protection, design quality, global market access. The language is exactly right. The execution will take time.

What Indonesia has that no government initiative can create from scratch is authenticity at scale. Batik eco-print techniques using natural dyes, raffia and rattan accessories coming out of Bali, handmade bags that are landing in New York and Sydney because ethical fashion buyers are actively looking for exactly what Indonesian artisans have always made.

The global market came to Indonesia before Indonesia had a name for what it was selling.
