The advice used to be unanimous. If you were building a technology company and you were serious about it, you moved to San Francisco. You took the meetings on Sand Hill Road. You learned to speak the language of the Valley and you optimised your pitch for partners who had never been to Southeast Asia and weren't entirely sure where it was.

That advice is no longer unanimous. And the founders who ignored it first are now the ones building the most interesting companies in the region.

## Anya Krishnamurthy, Bangalore

Anya Krishnamurthy has been offered term sheets by three of the top five Silicon Valley venture firms. She has turned down all of them.

"They wanted me to relocate," she says. "They also wanted me to build for a global market, which in practice meant building for the US market. I'm building for India. Those are different problems."

Her company, Farmchain, uses distributed ledger technology to create transparent supply chains for agricultural produce across South and Southeast Asia. The problem it solves does not exist in California. The solution it offers does not scale from California. Farmchain closed a $40 million Series B last year, led by Asian institutional investors, without a single dollar from the Valley.

## Marcus Lim, Kuala Lumpur

Marcus Lim spent two years in San Francisco before returning to Kuala Lumpur in 2021. "I understood what I was learning," he says. "But I also understood that what I was learning was how to build for a market I didn't want to build for."

His company, Payslip, is a payroll infrastructure business serving the specific complexity of Southeast Asian employment markets. It is, by any measure, an unsexy business. It is also profitable.

"The Valley funds exciting," Lim says. "Exciting usually means losing money for a long time. Infrastructure is boring. Infrastructure also works."

## Priya Osman, Jakarta

Priya Osman did the Valley thing — Stanford MBA, two years at a growth-stage startup. She came back to Jakarta not because she rejected the model but because she saw an opportunity that required being in Jakarta to see.

"There are forty million micro and small businesses in Indonesia. Almost none have access to formal financial services. You cannot understand this opportunity from San Francisco. The texture of it — you only get that by being here."

Her company, Toko Capital, has disbursed over $200 million in loans across Indonesia, with default rates that would make a Western fintech analyst do a double take.

## What Connects Them

What connects these founders is not a rejection of ambition — they are all building for scale. What connects them is a rejection of the assumption that the Valley's model of building, funding, and growing a company is the only model.

Asia is developing its own model. These are some of the people writing it.
