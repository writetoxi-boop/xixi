Karina stepped onto the steps in custom Prada and the fashion world clocked it immediately: a voluminous black satin overcoat cut to echo the silhouette of the Korean *durumagi*, draped over a white crystal-trimmed dress whose square neckline was a direct reference to the *hanbok* collar. This was not a stylist's romantic flourish. This was a negotiation. A European luxury house — one of the most controlled brands in the world — had been asked to serve traditional Korean sartorial architecture. And it said yes.

For a decade, the conversation around Asian stars at the Met has been shaped by a single question: *are they wearing the brands, or are the brands wearing them?* The framing has always been about access — about who gets the front row, the invite, the cover. But that's not what was happening on those steps this year.

This year, Asia was setting the brief.

Jisoo in Dior. Jennie in Chanel. Cai Xukun in something that looked like fractured dandyism given physical form. These were not brand placements. They were position papers. Each look was an argument about whose aesthetic vocabulary gets to define *art* — and the answer, increasingly, is not Paris alone.

The Met Gala has always been a power map disguised as a party. What it mapped in 2026 was simple: Asian cultural capital has crossed the threshold from influence to authorship. The brands are no longer just courting Asian stars. They are being reshaped by them.

That is a different thing entirely.
