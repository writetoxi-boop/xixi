Kuala Lumpur has held UNESCO Creative City of Design status since 2015. It is not a fact that travels well outside Malaysia. The city's global image has long been dominated by a handful of totems — the Petronas Towers, the street food, the transit efficiency — and the conversation about its creative life has largely stayed domestic. KL Fest is, among other things, a correction to that.

The 2026 edition is themed *Memory & Tomorrow* — how inherited stories shape imagined futures. The programming reflects it: *The Wayang Women*, a wayang kulit performance by an all-women troupe. *Siapa Cacat?*, a theatre production led by artists with disabilities. *Irama Pusaka*, three nights of heritage music in a concert hall. *Warung Terang*, which turns back alleyways into lit art installations. International touring works getting their Malaysian and Asian premieres.

This is not a government arts calendar. This is a city using culture as infrastructure — as the connective tissue between heritage districts and future ambitions, between older Malaysians who remember when these streets were the economic heart of the country and younger ones who are beginning to understand that those streets might be again.

The festival is also creating 800 jobs in Malaysia's creative sector. That number does not appear in most coverage. It should.

KL is not trying to be Singapore. It is not trying to be Bangkok. It is building something specific to what Kuala Lumpur actually is: a multilingual, multi-ethnic, architecturally layered city that has never quite gotten credit for how culturally rich it already is.
