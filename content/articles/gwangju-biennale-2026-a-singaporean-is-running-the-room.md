Sit with that for a moment.

The Gwangju Biennale was founded in 1995 as a direct memorial to the Gwangju Uprising of 1980 — one of the most significant events in South Korea's democratic history. It has always been an institution with weight. A Biennial that carries memory in its architecture, where the question of who gets to tell which story matters in ways that most art fairs never have to confront.

Ho Tzu Nyen is an artist who has spent his career working precisely at the intersection of historical memory, national mythology, and the politics of representation in Southeast Asia. His *One or Several Tigers* explored the tiger as a colonial and postcolonial symbol across the region. His work with The Critical Dictionary of Southeast Asia has catalogued the region's shared and contested iconographies for years.

He is, in other words, exactly the right person for this. Not because he is neutral — he is not — but because he understands that the deepest questions in Asian culture are questions about who controls the archive.

What his appointment signals is something larger than one hire: Asia's cultural institutions are becoming genuinely pan-Asian in their imagination. Not pan-Asian in the flattened, branding-exercise sense — but in the way that a Korean biennale trusts a Singaporean artist to hold one of its most significant curatorial responsibilities. This is what intra-Asian cultural exchange looks like when it is serious.

XIXI was built for exactly this moment. The moment when Asia stops narrating itself through Western frameworks and starts narrating itself through its own.
